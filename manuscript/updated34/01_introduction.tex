\section{Introduction}

Universities devote substantial resources to moving research into patents, licenses, and new firms. The formal rules that govern this activity are written into institutional intellectual-property (IP) policies, which specify who owns inventions, what must be disclosed, how income is shared, how conflicts of interest are handled, and what inventors can expect from the technology-transfer office (TTO). These documents are not static. Universities revise them in response to legal developments, governance priorities, organizational experience, and changes in how they manage commercialization. A natural empirical question is therefore: what happens to technology-transfer outcomes when a university rewrites its IP policy?

The literature gives only partial guidance. Some of the strongest within-system evidence concerns large changes in the ownership regime, most prominently Norway's abolition of the ``professor's privilege,'' which shifted ownership of university inventions from researchers to institutions and was followed by a large decline in university-researcher startups and patenting \citep{HvideJones2018}. Cross-institution studies relate specific contractual terms, especially inventor royalty shares, to commercialization \citep{LachSchankerman2008,ArqueCastellsEtAl2016}, although these relationships weaken when policy data are corrected and expanded \citep{OuelletteTutt2020}. Work on technology-transfer offices instead emphasizes staffing, routines, incentives, and relationships with firms \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,MarkmanEtAl2005,DebackereVeugelers2005}. What is largely missing is systematic evidence on the ordinary policy revisions that universities actually undertake, which often preserve institutional ownership while changing multiple provisions and the language surrounding them at once.

This paper studies those ordinary revisions. It uses the Policy Communication Stance Index (PCSI), developed and validated in a companion paper \citep{SharmaPCSI2026}, to characterize whether a revision moves policy language toward a more supportive or a more restrictive stance toward inventors. Rather than relying primarily on annual policy-in-force scores, most of which are carried forward from older documents, we focus on moments at which one observed policy document replaces another. Positive changes in PCSI are called \emph{upward} revisions and negative changes are called \emph{downward} revisions. The empirical question is whether technology-transfer outcomes evolve differently around those two kinds of revision after accounting for contemporaneous changes among non-revising universities.

A central contribution is a transparent sample-construction procedure. At the baseline threshold, the linked policy histories contain 127 revisions at 78 universities. A mechanical screen removes events with overlapping revisions in the event window or insufficient outcome support, leaving 62 potentially usable revisions. We then review the predecessor and successor documents for all 62 mechanically eligible events using common comparability and timing rules. Thirty-four revisions satisfy the documentary criteria and form the preferred sample; a stricter sensitivity sample also requires an observed calendar year after the revision. The resulting design therefore separates the complete revision universe, mechanical eligibility, and documentary usability rather than choosing events on the basis of estimated outcomes.

The main design is a stacked event study \citep{CengizEtAl2019,BakerLarckerWang2022}. Each retained revision defines an event-specific comparison set containing the revising university and contemporaneous universities without another threshold revision in the same event window. We estimate the same specification separately for five technology-transfer outcomes: licenses and options executed, a filing-margin measure, new patent applications, patents issued, and invention disclosures. The key contrast is the post-revision difference between upward and downward revisions relative to their event-specific controls.

The strongest pattern appears in licensing. Upward revisions are followed by higher licensing relative to clean controls, while downward revisions are followed by lower licensing. The differential is not statistically detectable before revision and is stable under a stricter post-support rule and leave-one-event-out analysis. Filing and new patent applications move in the same direction but with less precision; patents issued are weaker still; and we do not detect a corresponding increase in invention disclosures. The pattern therefore appears most clearly at the downstream licensing margin rather than at faculty entry into the formal disclosure process.

The licensing result should not be read as evidence that supportive wording itself causes commercialization. Revision direction is chosen by universities, not assigned. Upward revisions also begin from substantially less supportive prior policies, creating a serious selection and mean-reversion concern. Direction-label permutation inference is useful in a design with few upward events, but its exchangeability assumption is substantive rather than design-based. In addition, adjustment across the five main outcomes weakens the statistical evidence for licensing. We therefore interpret the estimates as a robust, outcome-specific post-revision pattern rather than a causal effect of policy language.

This paper contributes in two ways. Substantively, it studies a setting between cross-sectional comparisons of individual policy terms and large national ownership reforms: ordinary within-university policy revisions. Methodologically, it provides an auditable workflow for turning incomplete archival policy histories into dated event-study samples and makes the distinction between the revision universe, documentary review, and outcome estimation explicit. The design is informative about timing and the location of post-revision change along the technology-transfer pipeline, even though it cannot isolate policy wording from organizational changes that may accompany a rewrite.
