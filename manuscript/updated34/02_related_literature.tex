\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}

The Bayh--Dole Act allowed universities and other federal research contractors to elect to retain title to qualifying federally funded inventions, subject to federal rights and obligations. The resulting institutional role in managing university inventions made the design of university commercialization rules an important subject in the study of academic technology transfer \citep{MoweryEtAl2001,HendersonJaffeTrajtenberg1998,Sampat2006,GrimaldiEtAl2011}. A first body of evidence asks whether particular policy terms matter. Universities with higher inventor royalty shares have been found to earn more licensing income \citep{LachSchankerman2008}; willingness to take equity and lower royalty shares are associated with more startups \citep{DiGregorioShane2003}; and public universities with local-development objectives license differently from private institutions \citep{BelenzonSchankerman2009}. Related European evidence links royalty sharing to inventive effort \citep{ArqueCastellsEtAl2016}.

These studies are informative but face a basic comparability problem: universities that adopt different contractual terms also differ in organizational capacity, research portfolios, governance, and commercialization strategies. When policy data are corrected and expanded, the royalty-share relationship weakens considerably \citep{OuelletteTutt2020}, underscoring the difficulty of treating one observed contractual term as an isolated policy lever. Some of the strongest within-system evidence instead comes from reforms that change rules across a common institutional setting. \citet{HvideJones2018} show that when Norway transferred ownership of university inventions from researchers to institutions, startups and patenting by university researchers fell sharply, while \citet{GeunaRossi2011} document the broader European shift toward institutional ownership. \citet{KenneyPatton2009} argue that alternative ownership models may produce different commercialization incentives. These reforms, however, are unusually large: they change who holds the rights to an invention and therefore operate directly on researchers' incentives to commercialize.

Most university policy change is more incremental. Institutions periodically rewrite their IP policies while retaining the basic ownership regime, changing disclosure procedures, revenue sharing, conflict-of-interest rules, equity provisions, assignment language, appeal procedures, and the way the institution communicates with inventors. These are precisely the revisions that universities and state systems routinely make, yet they have rarely been studied as dated empirical events. Whether such revisions are followed by changes in commercialization, and at which stage of the process, remains an open question.

Where a revision might matter depends on how commercialization is organized. A large literature treats the TTO as the organizational locus of commercialization performance, with productivity that varies with staffing, incentive pay, routines, and relationships with industry \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,SiegelWaldmanAtwater2004,ThursbyKemp2002,MarkmanEtAl2005,OsheaEtAl2005}. TTOs are boundary-spanning organizations that translate between science, law, and markets \citep{DebackereVeugelers2005,PerkmannEtAl2013,HolgerssonEtAl2019}. Because many university inventions are licensed at an embryonic stage, commercialization also depends on continued cooperation among inventors, the office, and potential licensees \citep{JensenThursby2001}. Formal policy is one of the institutional instruments through which those relationships are governed.

This organizational perspective suggests at least two margins at which policy revision could be associated with subsequent outcomes. The first is faculty entry into the formal process. Many potentially commercial inventions are never disclosed \citep{JensenThursbyThursby2003}; disclosure depends on local norms, peers, and perceptions of the TTO \citep{OwenSmithPowell2001,BercovitzFeldman2008}; and part of the historical growth in university licensing has been attributed to faculty's increasing willingness to disclose \citep{ThursbyThursby2002}. If a revision changes how faculty perceive disclosure or ownership obligations, invention disclosures are a natural place to look for an early response. The second margin lies after disclosure, where the office evaluates inventions, decides whether to seek protection, markets technologies, and negotiates with firms. A revision associated with changes in these downstream relationships could appear in patenting or licensing without a corresponding increase in disclosure. Stage-specific measures therefore help locate where the post-revision pattern is strongest. The filing ratio of new patent applications to disclosures, for example, is intended to capture the decision to seek protection for what has been disclosed \citep{ChoudhryPonzio2020,Hall2021}; our institution-year filing margin is a log-difference analogue rather than an invention-level conversion probability.

Whether written policy itself matters is also theoretically ambiguous. Institutional theory allows formal structures to be ceremonial, adopted for legitimacy and partially decoupled from practice \citep{MeyerRowan1977}. Research on administrative burden instead emphasizes how learning, compliance, and psychological costs embedded in formal procedures can shape interactions with institutions \citep{MoynihanHerdHarvey2015,HerdMoynihan2018}. A policy rewrite may also be a visible record of organizational changes that are not contained in the text: new leadership, a reorganization of the TTO, altered commercialization priorities, or changes in university-industry strategy. Observing a post-revision outcome difference therefore cannot by itself distinguish a textual channel from organizational changes that travel with the rewrite.

The companion paper supplies a consistent measure of policy communication by applying text-as-data methods to institutional IP documents \citep{SharmaPCSI2026,LoughranMcDonald2011,GentzkowKellyTaddy2019,GrimmerRobertsStewart2022}. This paper uses that measure to characterize revision direction and links dated revisions to technology-transfer outcomes. Relative to cross-institution studies, the design compares universities around within-institution policy changes and with contemporaneous non-revisers. Relative to large ownership reforms, it studies the ordinary revisions universities choose. Relative to the TTO literature, the multiple outcomes let us ask whether the strongest post-revision pattern appears at faculty disclosure, patenting, or licensing. The contribution is therefore not a causal estimate of particular words or clauses, but systematic evidence on when ordinary policy rewrites occur and where subsequent technology-transfer outcomes diverge.
