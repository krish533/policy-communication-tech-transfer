\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The central empirical pattern is a divergence in licensing after ordinary university IP-policy revisions. In the preferred documentary-reviewed sample, revisions toward more supportive communication are followed by higher licensing relative to contemporaneous non-revisers, while revisions toward more restrictive communication are followed by lower licensing. The differential is not statistically detectable in the pre-revision event-time coefficients, remains similar under the stricter post-support rule, and stays positive when any single event is removed.

The revised sample construction matters for how this pattern should be read. Rather than beginning with a small hand-selected set of events, the current workflow starts from the full threshold-revision universe, applies mechanical non-overlap and outcome-support rules, and subjects every mechanically eligible pair to common documentary comparability and timing criteria. Expanding the documentary review reduces the magnitude of the licensing estimate relative to the earlier event set rather than strengthening it. This does not remove selection into revision direction, but it makes the path from the observed policy corpus to the preferred event sample substantially more transparent.

The evidence remains outcome-specific. Filing and patent applications move in the same direction as licensing but with considerably less precision, patents issued are weaker, and we do not detect a corresponding increase in invention disclosure. Moreover, the licensing result does not survive the reported Benjamini--Hochberg adjustment across the five principal outcomes. The paper therefore does not establish that more supportive revisions improve technology transfer generally. Its strongest empirical claim is narrower: the clearest post-revision divergence appears at the licensing margin.

That location is consistent with the literature that treats the TTO as an organization rather than a passive conduit. Licensing is the stage at which office staffing, routines, market search, negotiation, and relationships with firms matter most \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,ThursbyKemp2002,MarkmanEtAl2005}. It is also the stage at which continued cooperation among inventors, TTO staff, and prospective licensees is particularly important for embryonic university technologies \citep{JensenThursby2001}. Invention disclosure, by contrast, is closer to the faculty-entry margin emphasized by \citet{JensenThursbyThursby2003}, \citet{OwenSmithPowell2001}, and \citet{BercovitzFeldman2008}. The outcome pattern therefore helps locate where the association appears, even though the data cannot identify why it appears there.

The content evidence is consistent with an organizational interpretation but remains secondary. Across the full revision universe, downward revisions are more likely to add conflict-of-interest language. Such language concerns faculty relationships with outside firms and could be associated with more complex commercialization procedures, but keyword presence does not establish legal meaning or administrative burden. Provision-level coding of the original content-coded subset likewise suggests differences in several clauses, but those labels were not retroactively extended to the newly admitted events after observing the expanded-sample results. Independent provision coding would be needed to test specific textual or legal mechanisms credibly.

The main identification problem is not timing but selection into revision direction. Upward and downward revisions have similar pre-revision licensing trends, yet upward revisions start from markedly less supportive predecessor policies. This imbalance may reflect mean reversion, institutional heterogeneity, or systematically different reasons for revising. A university that moves in a supportive direction may also be changing TTO leadership, budgets, workflows, or commercialization priorities. The stacked design, fixed effects, research-expenditure control, clean comparison sets, pre-trend tests, and permutation inference all improve the comparison, but none makes revision direction exogenous. The direction-label permutation procedure is therefore best understood as an inferential sensitivity under exchangeability, not as design-based randomization inference.

The distinction between policy text and policy episode is especially important. Institutional theory allows formal rules to be decoupled from practice \citep{MeyerRowan1977}, while the administrative-burden literature provides reasons why written procedures could shape behavior \citep{MoynihanHerdHarvey2015,HerdMoynihan2018}. The evidence here rules out neither possibility. A documented rewrite may itself matter, or it may be the visible component of a broader organizational reform. Because the current data do not observe all accompanying changes, the estimated post-revision contrast should be interpreted as a property of revision episodes rather than a structural effect of wording.

For universities and state systems, the policy implication is correspondingly restrained. The results suggest that ordinary IP-policy revisions merit attention beyond administrative housekeeping because revision episodes coincide with economically meaningful changes in licensing outcomes and the direction of communication contains information about where those changes appear. They do not show that rewriting a policy in more supportive language will mechanically increase licensing. A stronger causal design would require plausibly exogenous variation---for example, a governing-system mandate, staggered campus adoption of a common reform, or a legal shock with differential exposure.

Several data limitations also bound the conclusions. The preferred sample contains few upward revisions; some event dates rely on tier-B documentary timing; AUTM reporting is voluntary; outcomes are institution-level aggregates; the survey does not follow individual inventions from disclosure to licensing; and the 2023 endpoint limits post-revision support for the latest events. These constraints motivate the stricter post-support sample and the paper's emphasis on the location and timing of the observed pattern rather than on a causal magnitude.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies what happens around ordinary revisions of university intellectual-property policies. By combining a text-based measure of policy communication with documentary review and AUTM technology-transfer outcomes, it constructs an auditable set of dated revision events and compares upward and downward revisions with contemporaneous non-revisers in a stacked event-study framework.

The clearest post-revision divergence appears in licensing. Filing and patent-application measures point in the same direction with less precision, while we do not detect a corresponding increase in invention disclosure. The pattern is stable to stricter post-event support and leave-one-event-out analysis, but it weakens under multiple-testing adjustment and is subject to a substantial prior-PCSI imbalance between upward and downward revisions.

The contribution is therefore not a causal estimate of policy wording. It is evidence on ordinary within-university policy-revision episodes, evidence on where along the technology-transfer pipeline the strongest post-revision difference appears, and a transparent method for turning incomplete policy archives into empirical events. Future work should seek reforms whose timing or direction is externally induced and should independently code the legal and organizational changes that accompany policy rewrites.
